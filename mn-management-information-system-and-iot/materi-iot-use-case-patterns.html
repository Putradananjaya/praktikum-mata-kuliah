IoT Use Case Patterns, Business Value, and Readiness

Session 4 explained how an IoT deployment works. This session asks a managerial question instead: where is it worth deploying at all? No coding and no hardware are involved; the decisions here are business decisions.

Meet PAMN, Your IoT Advisor

PAMN is the robot who looks after Toko Kopi Nusantara. Today PAMN advises four kinds of businesses in Bali that are considering IoT: a minimarket chain, a distribution company, a coffee plantation in Kintamani, and a resort hotel.

PAMN does not ask "Which sensor is the newest?" PAMN asks three questions: Which pattern is this? What value can we prove? Is the organisation ready to act? This page follows the same three questions.

Learning Objectives

After this session you should be able to:

  1. Compare IoT use cases across at least three industry sectors. C4
  2. Identify the business value generated by each use case in measurable terms. C4
  3. Assess the readiness conditions required before IoT adoption is feasible. C5

Chapter 1

The Five Patterns

Nearly every IoT use case belongs to one of five patterns. Each pattern is defined by the question it answers, not by the sensor it uses. Two use cases that answer the same question are the same pattern, however different their sector or hardware looks.

The animation cycles through the five patterns, about five seconds each.

Only Predictive Maintenance has a built-in delay between deployment and benefit, because a baseline of normal behaviour must be learned first. A proposal promising immediate returns from it is not credible.

The Design Test

Three use cases are genuinely different only if they belong to three different patterns. If two share the same core question, they are the same pattern, however different the sector or sensor appears. A proposal listing "chiller temperature, cargo temperature, and greenhouse humidity" offers one idea three times.

Chapter 3

Four Layers, Carried Over from Session 4

Every deployment has the same four layers. A managerial proposal should state each one in plain language.

1 · Device

What is measured, and where the sensor sits. Placement follows the business question, not maintenance convenience.

2 · Connectivity

How the data leaves the site. Chosen by physical constraint: distance, power, and mobility.

3 · Platform

The rule that turns readings into an event, including a tolerance window, not just a threshold.

4 · Application

Who receives what, and which decision it supports: an immediate alert and a durable report.

Connectivity is driven by physical constraint (distance, power, mobility), not by preference or familiarity.

Connectivity Decision Rules

  1. Does the asset move? If yes, Wi-Fi and Ethernet are ruled out.
  2. Is there mains power? If no, Wi-Fi and cellular are ruled out.
  3. How far from infrastructure? If kilometres, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and Ethernet are ruled out.

Chapter 4

Four Sources of Business Value

Business value comes from four sources. Their evidentiary strength is not equal.

Compliance Changes the Question

Without a regulatory obligationWith a regulatory obligation
"Is this worth it?" A cost-benefit judgment."What is the cheapest compliant option?"

This is the regulatory pressure from Session 3: it applies regardless of competitive position or profitability.

The Stated-Basis Rule

An honest unknown with a measurement plan beats a confident fabrication.
ClaimVerdictWhy
"Improves efficiency by 40%."UnacceptableNo stated basis; cannot be verified or tracked; collapses when anyone asks where it came from.
"Currently 8% of order lines stock out; this is the figure the investment must move."AcceptableTied to a measurable current baseline.
"We cannot estimate the revenue effect reliably, but monthly spoilage cost is the metric to track."AcceptableAn honest unknown with a measurement plan.

The rule applies to the group assignment, the midterm, and the final project.

Chapter 5

Readiness: Three Conditions

A use case failing any one of these conditions is not ready, however attractive the technology is.

The Master Test

"If all three sensors work perfectly tomorrow, who does what differently?"

A passing answer names a role, a specific action, and a time window. Anything vaguer means the use case is not ready.

Where IoT Does Not Fit

Game: Readiness Gate

Each proposal must pass three gates in order. Judge every gate. If a gate truly fails, the proposal stops there. Proposals that pass all three meet the final boss question.

Teaser: Weeks 13–14

The hotel occupancy sensor that saves energy also tells management when a guest is in their room. Presence and Flow is the only pattern whose subject is people. How Indonesia's Personal Data Protection Law (UU 27/2022) shapes such designs is treated fully in Weeks 13–14.

Summary

Session 5 in Five Points

  1. Five patterns cover nearly all IoT use cases. Recognising the pattern transfers; memorising the example does not.
  2. Business value has four sources with descending evidentiary strength, and compliance changes the question from whether to which cheapest option.
  3. Every value claim needs a stated basis. An honest unknown with a measurement plan beats a confident fabrication.
  4. Recurring cost scales with device count, the line proposals most often omit.
  5. Three readiness conditions: capacity to act, environmental suitability, cost proportionality. Technology attractiveness is not among them.
